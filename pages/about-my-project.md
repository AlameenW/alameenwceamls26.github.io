problem: |
  After a disaster — a hurricane, earthquake, or flood — someone has to figure out which buildings are safe to enter, which are restricted, and which are too dangerous to go near. Right now, that means sending inspectors out         physically, one building at a time. It's slow, resource-intensive, and in the immediate aftermath of a disaster, potentially dangerous for the inspectors themselves.
  This delay has real consequences. Emergency responders, city officials, and displaced residents are left waiting on critical information about whether it's safe to return home or reenter a building. The longer that takes, the      slower recovery gets.
  Our project aims to close that gap by using aerial imagery and AI to automate the initial damage classification — flagging buildings as safe, restricted, or dangerous — before a single inspector sets foot on the ground. The goal   is to give decision-makers a faster, scalable first look so that resources and personnel can be directed where they're needed most.

outcome: |
  A trained damage classification model — a fine-tuned CNN that can take an aerial building image and return a Green/Yellow/Red classification consistent with ATC-20 standards. Other researchers should be able to build on it,        retrain it on new disaster datasets, or benchmark it against their own approaches.
  A working web application — a mobile-compatible interface where a user uploads a building image and gets a damage assessment back with a confidence score. 
  A curated dataset — 500+ labeled aerial building images across the three damage categories, cleaned and split for training, validation, and testing. This alone has value as a resource for future post-disaster AI research.
  A research poster and presentation — delivered at the SAIRI Research Symposium, documenting the full pipeline, methodology, results, and comparison against manual ATC-20 assessments.
  The bigger hope is that the app and model together serve as a proof-of-concept that remote, AI-assisted damage assessment is viable — something that could inform how emergency management agencies think about deploying this kind    of tool after real disasters.
